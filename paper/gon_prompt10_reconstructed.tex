\begin{abstract}
We examine two closed finite repair grammars: one supports name-orbit quotient search only under permutation-invariant weighted semantics, and another supports conservative decomposition only under verified independent action footprints. The frozen benchmark comprises 72 synthetic instances; 36 matched comparison cells and 36 required-refusal controls. A resource-limited 20-repetition rerun completed 1,440 measured trials with zero censored outcomes. Quotient search was slower on the tested small symmetry instances. No novel foundational theorem or real-world superiority is claimed.
\end{abstract}
\section{Problem definition and scope}
A general repair state records components, dependencies, status and relevant history. Applicable deterministic actions have nonnegative costs, and a feasible plan must reach a goal, avoid forbidden configurations and respect a budget. The implementation does not support unrestricted graph rewriting. Its single-service grammar supports create, rewire and restore actions over finite replica identities; identity-sensitive costs and opaque guards forbid quotienting. Its separate multi-component grammar supports local restart, create and rewire operations. Shared capacity, cross edges, shared allocation and global guards forbid decomposition. Cross edges are refusal flags, not executed coupled-graph semantics.
\section{Conditional correctness}
Standard Dijkstra uniform-cost search is optimal on a finite nonnegative weighted graph if its state key preserves all future-relevant information. For a finite permutation group preserving action applicability, costs, transitions and goal truth, exact orbit identification preserves optimal cost. The implemented finite orbit certificate is restricted to the closed grammar, not a general proof engine. If the global transition system is an asynchronous product of local systems, all action read/write/guard/allocation footprints are independent, costs are additive and the goal conjunctive, projection and concatenation prove
\[C^*=\sum_{i=1}^{n}C_i^*.\]
An external budget B permits a solution iff the sum is at most B. This is standard separability, not a novel theorem.
\section{Falsified conjectures and standard lemma}
Shared restart capacity invalidates naive additivity: two components with restart cost 1 and create-plus-rewire cost 3 each have unconstrained optimum sum 2, but at most one restart gives optimum 4. Selecting the lowest absolute restart cost can fail: restart/alternative pairs (1,1) and (2,4), with one restart, give cost 5 if the first is restarted but 3 if the second is restarted. For independent binary choices with at most k restarts, set d_i=q_i-r_i. The optimal cost is
\[C^*=\sum_iq_i-\sum_{j=1}^{\min(k,m)}d^+_{(j)},\]
where the latter are the descending positive savings. An exchange argument proves this standard cardinality-greedy lemma, implemented by sorting in O(n log n). Deterministic exhaustive checks covered 80,973 parameter cases.
\section{Frozen synthetic benchmark}
The generator version gon-benchmark-v1.0.0 uses eight strata, seeds 17, 29, 43, sizes 2, 3, 4, and canonical instance/topology SHA-256 digests. Nine fresh-symmetry cells permit plain versus quotient UCS; 27 independent/local-history/local-negative cells permit full versus decomposed UCS; 36 other cells test conservative refusal. Each supported cost is checked against an independent exhaustive oracle and each plan replayed. The isolated rerun uses three warmups, 20 paired repetitions per algorithm/instance, a 60-second subprocess timeout, and a Linux 2 GiB virtual-address-space cap. All 1,440 trials succeeded and none were censored.

\section{Limitations and availability}

Virtual-address-space limits are not RSS measurements.
